The session opened in fragments of memory and history.

Orivon had a scene of his own, somewhere behind the shape of the man he had become. Demingo did as well. Whatever truths were shown there remained personal, half-buried beneath the immediate trouble that followed.

Because by morning, Demingo had become the problem.

She woke in the city beside a man who looked dead.

The details were unclear, even to her. There was a body. There was hunger. There was the aftermath of something she had done or something she could not fully remember doing. Whatever had happened, she had been seen fleeing the scene before making her way back to the tavern.

When morning came, most of the party had already gathered downstairs.

Demingo was not with them at first. Neither was Orivon.

Then Demingo came down.

She looked different.

Refreshed. Younger. Healthier. Almost radiant, as if years of wear had been pulled away from her overnight. But whatever had restored her body had done nothing to ease her mind. Guilt sat plainly across her face.

The Zhelezo arrived soon after.

They came into the tavern looking specifically for Demingo. Once they found her, they moved with practiced authority, forming a perimeter and making clear that this was not a casual question. Demingo had been seen with a dead body.

Orivon stood against them.

He argued, pushed back, and tried to keep the situation from closing around her, but there was only so far words could carry him in a room full of armed guards. Eventually, the Zhelezo stripped Demingo of her weapons and took her away.

The tavern was left quieter after that.

Not calmer.

Just quieter.

---

Orivon asked the bartender if he knew of a noble who might be able to speak on Demingo’s behalf. In a city like Nicodranas, law and power were not always separate things. The right name could matter as much as the truth.

The bartender pointed them uptown, toward the wealthier shops and richer streets.

Ellyanora had been present, but distant. The arrest seemed to pass around her more than through her. Either she had been lost in her own thoughts, or mortal law simply did not hold her attention the way divine threats did.

Outside, the city had already gathered around the evidence.

A body lay in the street.

It looked drained of life.

A noble and a wizard circled it, examining what remained while the Zhelezo held a line around the scene. Beyond that line, a crowd had formed, thick with fear and speculation. People were watching because people always watched, but none of them looked eager to step closer.

There was not much the party could gain there.

So they went north.

---

The richer part of town had better stone, cleaner windows, and people who expected money before cooperation.

The party stopped at a nice bakery. The man behind the counter was not especially interested in giving information freely, and it became clear quickly enough that if they wanted him helpful, they would need to make the exchange worth his time.

Aporia bought two donuts.

For forty gold.

It was an absurd price, but apparently enough to make the man more willing to speak.

Aldric stepped forward to ask questions, then stopped himself. He was not incapable of speaking, but he knew what shape he carried in a room. Some people persuaded. Some people performed. Some people filled the space behind the speaker and made the speaker harder to ignore.

Aldric was better suited to the last one.

So he let Orivon do the talking and settled into the role more natural to him: silent weight at Orivon’s back.

The baker pointed them two doors down, toward a lacquered building where they could find someone like a lawyer.

He warned them it would not be cheap.

Given the price of the donuts, that warning carried weight.

---

The lawyer’s office was occupied by a tired half-elf somewhere between thirty and forty, though exhaustion made the number difficult to trust. His glasses sat low on his nose, and he seemed to be working through a court speech that was not improving with repetition.

When the party entered, he looked at them and assumed the worst.

Enforcers, perhaps. A noble’s men. A gang’s men. Something with muscle and money and a problem that would become his if he refused too quickly.

He wanted coin.

That, at least, was simple.

His rate was fifty gold for the retainer, then ten gold an hour after that. The party paid him enough to get him moving, and soon the scene shifted from his office to the place where Demingo was being held.

---

Demingo was being interrogated.

The noble and the wizard worked her with the familiar rhythm of pressure and release. One pushed. One measured. Good guard, bad guard, or some polished variation of it.

Demingo did not hold well under it.

She started to break, words spilling out less like a defense and more like panic trying to find language.

Then the lawyer arrived.

He burst into the interrogation, pushed the others out, and took control of the room with the desperate confidence of a man who was finally useful. The party came in after him, and Ellyanora cast Silence to keep what followed from leaving the room.

Demingo told them she was a vampire.

Then, almost as importantly to her, she clarified that she had not slept with Ellyanora the night before.

Ellyanora corrected the first part.

Demingo was not an undead vampire. Not truly. She was something vampire-like, something that drained physical vitality, beauty, youth, strength, or some other living asset from those she fed upon.

The distinction did not make the dead man less dead.

Ellyanora believed that stopping Demingo from drinking had likely forced the hunger elsewhere. Denied one outlet, it had found another. The result was the body in the street and Demingo in chains.

It was not a clean explanation.

It was still an explanation.

---

Ellyanora chose a solution that only she could have considered reasonable.

If Demingo had been imprisoned during a second, similar attack, then she could not be responsible for both.

So Ellyanora staged one.

While Demingo remained in custody, Ellyanora forced the shape of another crime into existence elsewhere. Then she wiped the lawyer’s memory of the more dangerous details: the vampire talk, the nature of Demingo’s hunger, and anything else that could point too clearly toward the truth.

When the noble returned with the wizard, the wizard looked furious.

The noble declared Demingo free to go.

Only later did the party understand what Ellyanora had done. She had stormed through the manor, nearly drained the noble, and left guards injured in her wake. The noble had survived, but only barely enough to be useful.

It worked.

That was not the same as it being good.

---

The party left with Demingo free, if not cleared in any way that mattered to conscience.

The lawyer left as well.

Or tried to.

Debt collectors caught him almost immediately.

Henrik, perhaps deciding the situation needed one more push in the wrong direction, told them the party had paid the lawyer four hundred gold. They had not. They had paid closer to a hundred.

The debt collectors dragged the lawyer away.

Nicodranas was becoming less comfortable by the moment.

So the party discussed leaving.

There were arguments for staying, for finding more answers, for searching out a larger library to the north. But the city had eyes on them now, and Demingo’s situation had already drawn too much attention.

They chose the barge to Port Damali.

Six days on the water offered distance.

Distance sounded useful.

---

Ellyanora got a penthouse.

This surprised no one.

The others took mid-tier rooms where they could, except for Henrik and Aldric, who ended up in the lower bunks near the bottom of the ship. It was cramped, plain, and probably smelled worse than the upper decks.

It suited them well enough.

Aporia went to speak with Ellyanora.

Orivon found the gambling hall and performed there. Demingo followed him, and the two played music together, letting the noise and motion of the ship swallow some of the day’s uglier edges.

Henrik and Aldric sparred on the deck.

It was practical. Familiar. A way to move tension through muscle rather than words. Henrik also spent time drawing a map, putting shape to places and paths while the coastline slipped behind them.

For a brief stretch, the barge offered something close to order.

Then a maid approached Henrik and Aldric.

She tried to convince them to steal from Ellyanora.

It was a poor choice of marks.

Henrik grabbed her, called the guards, and turned her in. Afterward, he and Aldric went to Ellyanora to report what had happened and collect payment.

Ellyanora gave each of them a gold bar.

Then she threw seven more gold bars over the side of the barge.

The gesture was so wasteful it was almost instructional.

Ellyanora did not think about wealth like ordinary people did. Gold, to her, was not survival, status, or security. It was simply metal with value assigned by people who had not seen the world from her height.

Or perhaps she was just making a point.

Either way, the bars vanished into the water.

---

Ellyanora also made a construct.

Like many things she did, it seemed to arrive with more implications than explanation.

It was not merely a servant. It moved with purpose and carried itself like a thing made to learn. A thing made to act. A thing that might become more dangerous the longer it was allowed to study what stood against it.

That would become clear soon enough.

But before that, Aldric and Demingo spoke on the deck.

Demingo came to him.

Earlier, she had told Aldric to put her down if she killed someone else. After the body in Nicodranas, after the arrest, after the hunger had found another victim, she came to face what that meant.

Aldric did not give her the mercy she asked for.

He called her a coward.

Not because she was afraid. Fear was understandable. Shame was understandable. Horror at what she had done was understandable.

But giving up was easier than changing.

Asking someone else to end her was easier than learning control.

Aldric had seen this shape before in other people. The pattern of harm, regret, apology, and repetition. The moment where someone wanted the consequence to be dramatic enough that they would not have to do the quieter, uglier work of becoming different.

He did not accept it from Demingo.

If she was dangerous, then she needed to learn what danger lived in her and how to master it. If she had killed, then the answer was not to dress surrender as responsibility and hand the burden to someone else.

She tried to convince him otherwise.

She failed.

Aldric did not move easily once he had decided something.

And on this, he had decided.

---

Later, Ellyanora’s construct approached Henrik.

It drew its blade toward him.

Henrik answered as Henrik did.

The two sparred, steel and motion cutting through the rhythm of the ship. At first, the construct was simply dangerous. Then it became something worse.

It improved.

The longer Henrik fought, the more the construct adapted. It learned from him, adjusted to him, and pressed harder with each exchange. Every motion became information. Every defense became something to test. Every mistake became a lesson the construct would not forget.

Then Henrik let his guard down.

The construct struck him down.

It did not finish him.

Instead, it reached down, pulled him back to his feet, bowed, and left.

The session ended there, on the deck of the barge, with Nicodranas behind them and six days of water ahead.

Demingo was free, but not innocent.

Ellyanora had saved her, but not cleanly.

Aldric had refused to make death an escape from change.

And somewhere beneath all of it, the cost of hunger remained unpaid.
